<section class="part" aria-labelledby="orpheus">
  <p class="part-num">I.</p>
  <h2 class="part-title" id="orpheus">Orpheus, Looking Back</h2>

I went down singing because singing was the only thing I had that the dead would stop for. People forget that part. The lyre was the ticket. The ferryman listened. The dog listened. The wheel stopped turning. Whatever I carried down there, I carried on the strength of a song. And the song was true, which is why it worked, and why it was dangerous. A true song makes you believe you've earned what it buys.

What I carried down was a life I was certain about. Pick the one that cost the most; that's the one I mean. I walked it into the dark on purpose, the way you carry a body you're not ready to bury, and I argued with the house of the dead for it, and I won. Hades gave his terms. Walk up. Don't turn. She follows.

And she did. I need to say that plainly, because the myth skips it: it was working. The whole stair up she was there, a second torch behind me, heat on the neck, light thrown past my shoulder onto the next step. I had everything I'd asked for, in the only form it was ever going to come in. Unconfirmed.

The look back happened at the lip of the light, and it didn't feel like doubt. It felt like love. It felt like checking on her. That's how the turn gets you: not with fear, with tenderness, and underneath the tenderness a demand. Prove it. Show me you're real before I've paid the whole price. I turned around to collect early on a debt that was still being honored, and the collecting is what voided it. Virgil has the riverbanks repeating her name down the whole length of the Hebrus. I know that sound. It's your own voice coming back.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">
  <p lang="la">Eurydicen vox ipsa et frigida lingua,<br />
  a miseram Eurydicen! anima fugiente vocabat:<br />
  Eurydicen toto referebant flumine ripae.</p>
  <p class="translation">"Eurydice," the voice itself, the tongue already cold, called with fleeing breath, "ah, poor Eurydice!" and "Eurydice" the banks gave back along the whole stream.</p>
  <cite>Virgil, Georgics IV.525–527</cite>
</blockquote>

Here's what the story doesn't tell you, because it ends on the river. He didn't lose her at the turn. He lost her the moment he stopped trusting the terms. The turn is just when it became visible. Every mark I carry reads the same way once you know how to read it: I lost it when I needed proof.

<blockquote class="pull-quote poem-quote">
  <p>I am done turning around.<br />
  Orpheus lost her checking for proof,<br />
  but I have learned to walk the long stair up<br />
  reading the dark itself.</p>
  <cite><a href="/gallery/pharmakon/">Pharmakon, VI</a></cite>
</blockquote>

</section>

<section class="part" aria-labelledby="guide">
  <p class="part-num">II.</p>
  <h2 class="part-title" id="guide">The Guide</h2>

Every descent needs a guide who has mapped the dark and kept his composure in it. Virgil walks ahead through his own poems first: the bees of the Georgics fail, and the beekeeper is sent down after the reason.

I didn't pick Virgil. He was already walking when I got there, and he had the only map with the lower floors on it. That's what a guide is. Not a teacher. Somebody who went down, kept his composure, and came back with his handwriting still legible.

He gets the bees back by sending the beekeeper to the source of the dying, not to a cure. That's the method. When something you built fails, the dead have the reason, and you go down and ask. You don't patch. You don't rebrand. You descend. And he plants Orpheus in the middle of that story on purpose, a warning posted at the trailhead: here is a man who went down for the right reason and came up wrong.

What nobody tells you about the descent is that you go down for one thing and the dark has its own agenda. I went down for a life I'd lost. What was waiting at the bottom wasn't the life. It was her. The guide's job is to get you that far and no farther. Virgil stops at the edge of what he has seen, and the honest ones always do. Past that line you walk it alone, with her behind you, and you do not turn around. I've had guides like that. I try to be one. Go first. Keep your handwriting legible. Know where your map runs out.

</section>

<section class="part" aria-labelledby="kindly-ones">
  <p class="part-num">III.</p>
  <h2 class="part-title" id="kindly-ones">The Kindly Ones</h2>

The Erinyes rise for blood-guilt inside the family: they answer wrongs done to kin, and they are older than the Olympian order that tries to civilize them. Athens renamed them Eumenides and gave them a home under the hill. The debt itself remains payable.

Here is what pursues. Not the law. The law can be argued with and outlasted. What pursues is older than the law and takes no appeals: the wrong done inside the house. The Erinyes don't need a corpse. They need blood on blood, and every house has it.

Mine goes back further than most. I spent my teens in a valley my fourth great-grandfather settled with his several wives, under the one god, in the order the one god prescribed. Five generations deep in that soil. And the wrong inside my house is the same wrong inside nearly every house on earth now: a boy cut on his first day by people who loved him, because their fathers were cut, because the book said so. No one should be born into a world of sexual violence, and almost all of us were. That's the blood-guilt. Not a crime one person committed. A practice the whole house carries, and carries forward, and calls love.

They hunt by smell. You can change cities and names and the dogs still find you, because what they're tracking is on you, not behind you. I ran for a while. Everyone does. It is the most exhausting thing a man can do and it doesn't work, which is why the oldest plays are about the running and not the crime.

Settlement looks like Athens. Not acquittal. Orestes stands trial, the vote ties, the goddess breaks it, and the Furies are not banished. They're given a home under the hill, a new name, and an honest share of the city, and in exchange they stop hunting. You don't beat them. You house them. You pull the practice out at the root in your own line. You put your own beliefs on the altar first, and you sit with the possibility that you fell for the oldest trick ever played on men. Then you pay on the debt, in public, for as long as it takes. The Kindly Ones is not irony. It's what they become when a man stops running and starts digging.

</section>

<section class="part" aria-labelledby="marks">
  <p class="part-num">IV.</p>
  <h2 class="part-title" id="marks">Marks on the Soul</h2>

The judges read the soul the way a physician reads a body, because every act leaves tissue. The scars are legible. And the old sources agree on the grading: wrongs against kin and against nature cut deepest, and greed stains deepest of all. It dyes the fabric through, because greed is the one sin a man commits continuously.

<blockquote class="pull-quote">
  <p lang="la">quisque suos patimur Manis.</p>
  <p class="translation">Each of us suffers his own shade.</p>
  <cite>Virgil, Aeneid VI.743</cite>
</blockquote>

<blockquote class="pull-quote">
  <p>"Your greed stains your soul with the stench of ruin."</p>
  <cite>D. C. A. Hillman, after Aeschylus</cite>
</blockquote>

The Greek underneath is exact. <span lang="grc">Δυσωδία</span>, the stench. <span lang="grc">Ἀπώλεια</span>, the ruin. Pollution has an odor in the old cosmology, and the Kindly Ones hunt by smell. The stained man carries it into every room he enters, and the dogs of the elder law can track it across any distance and any number of years.

<blockquote class="pull-quote poem-quote">
  <p>…the little dying<br />
  that drops the man I was<br />
  like a snake drops the winter skin<br />
  in the tall grass, and does not mourn it</p>
  <cite><a href="/gallery/pharmakon/">Pharmakon, III</a></cite>
</blockquote>

I've learned to read marks on the living. Watch anyone long enough, a politician, a preacher, a hero, a villain, and the costume slips, and underneath it's just a dude, scarred the same ways as everybody. The useful question shifts from <em>who is this person</em> to <em>what marked them, and what are they still paying on.</em> Then turn the same reading on yourself, while the marks can still be worked on. Do the examination before the judges do.

</section>

<section class="part" aria-labelledby="feather">
  <p class="part-num">V.</p>
  <h2 class="part-title" id="feather">The Feather</h2>

In the Egyptian hall of the Two Truths the heart is set on one pan of the balance and the feather of Ma'at on the other. The heart that weighs the same as truth passes; the heavy heart is given to the devourer. The scale is exact, and the feather is the whole standard.

The scale doesn't care what you did. That's the part the preachers miss. Anubis isn't reading a ledger. He weighs a heart against a feather, and the heart either weighs that or it doesn't. The forty-two declarations the dead recite in the hall are all negatives. I have not. I have not. You don't earn your way across. You arrive light or you don't.

So the question stops being what did I do and becomes what am I still carrying. Every mark on this page has a weight, and the weight is never the wrong itself. It's the unpaid part. The running. The needing proof. The debt without a room. The parts of yourself you never looked at because they were cut away before you could. Those are what a heart gets heavy with, and the devourer is patient, and the feather is exact.

I've stopped trying to get lighter by subtraction. You can't cut enough out; that was the whole lie. What makes a heart light is being seen in full, not broken, by the one who lives behind it. She does the weighing long before Anubis does. It comes in waves. Some days the pan drops. Then you build a place big enough to set things down in. A hearth. Wide. Where the dead can sit and the living can sit and nobody has to fold to fit. That's where the weighing resolves, if it resolves: not in the hall, in the house. Which is where <a href="/writings/monsoon/">the rain comes in</a>.

<blockquote class="pull-quote poem-quote">
  <p>I am building the hearth wide. Wide enough<br />
  for two infinities to sit down without folding.</p>
  <cite><a href="/gallery/pharmakon/">Pharmakon, VI</a></cite>
</blockquote>

</section>
