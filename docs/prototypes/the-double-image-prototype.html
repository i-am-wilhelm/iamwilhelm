A scrolly on seeing twice

The Double Image

One set of lines. Two pictures. The one you get depends on what you brought.

I · Figure and ground

Start with a column. Two vertical lines and nothing between them but paper.

Add a bump. A profile is a sum of bumps — brow, nose, lips, chin — and each bump is a bell curve on a line. Watch the nose arrive.

Fill the space between the lines. A vase. You would put flowers in it.

Fill the space outside the lines instead. Two faces, close enough to kiss.

Nothing moved. The ink changed sides. Edgar Rubin published this flip in 1915, and every double image since runs on the same hinge: figure and ground trade places, and the picture you see is the one you decided was the thing.

II · The paranoiac eye

In 1929 a young Catalan painter met Jacques Lacan, whose thesis was on paranoia: the mind's habit of reading intent into everything. Dalí took it as a gift.

He called the result the paranoiac-critical method: simulate the delusion, keep the sanity, and paint what the delusion sees with photographic precision. Breton's surrealists let the unconscious write. Dalí interrogated it.

A flock is just a flock.
Unless you are afraid enough.

That is the move in The Great Paranoiac, The Endless Enigma, Metamorphosis of Narcissus: one set of forms, two or three readings, and a hand steady enough to make every reading convincing. Freud, who had no patience for surrealists, met Dalí in London in 1938 and made an exception.

III · Letters first

Arcimboldo did it with fruit in the 1560s. Dalí did it with rocks and bathers. A screen does it with characters.

Every image on this site passes through a glyph field: a grid of Greek letters whose density is brightness. Up close, an alphabet. At distance, a face. Monet's broken color, set in type.

The function has not changed since the vase. Same bumps, same curve. The ink picked the other side, and the two faces that met across the vase now face away from each other.

That is Janus. The double image as a god.

The stars were letters first.

IV · The hinge

Here is the thing about a double image: the picture never changes. You do.

Scroll-driven art is the first medium where that is literally true. The reader's thumb is the paranoiac eye, and the threshold where one reading becomes the other is a line the author gets to place.

Stop scrolling.
It keeps turning anyway.

Tap the image to choose a side.

Two readings,
one hand.

Dalí described his method as irrational knowledge held to critical standards. Strip the mysticism and the recipe is plain: pick an ordinary thing, let the interpretive drive run until it finds a second thing inside the first, then render the hinge so cleanly that both survive. Rubin's vase is the proof of concept. Goya's masked satires, Arcimboldo's fruit, Varo's workshops, the Polish poster school — all of them are double images with the second reading reserved for whoever brought the key.

This page is one. Every picture on it is the same function wearing different ink.
