Free Won’t Closer

By Michael WilhelmNovember 2026

Free Won'tFreeWillWon’t

A nervous system can be hijacked. That was already true in 1879. This is about what survives the hijack, where it lives, and the thing on a long orbit that keeps getting closer.

YouSomething on a long orbit

“We are now hackable animals.”

Yuval Noah Harari, Davos, January 2020

Scroll. It is already moving.

I

The Hack Is Old

Chapter I of V1879 to now

This line is you. One nervous system, humming along, running its own rhythm.

1879 · Menlo Park

Edison’s lamp hands the night to anyone with a switch. Light after dark holds back melatonin, the hormone that tells the body it is night.

Nobody voted on the new bedtime.

1929 · Fifth Avenue

Edward Bernays, Freud’s nephew, sends young women into New York’s Easter parade to light cigarettes for the cameras. The press is told to watch for torches of freedom. The client is American Tobacco.

Marketing is just that. An idea gets planted, and it feels like your own.

The body

For most of a century, leaded gasoline, paint and pipes dose children with a neurotoxin tied to weaker impulse control. Food labs tune sweetness to a bliss point, the level where the hand keeps going back to the bag.

Chemistry sets the mood before you form an opinion.

The feed

Then the screen. The feed pays out on a slot machine’s schedule, built to keep the thumb moving.

You are scrolling right now. Notice that.

The sensors

The phone sells its own trail. Brokers have traded the movements of hundreds of millions of devices, fine enough to follow one to a clinic or a church.

The television can capture what is on its screen hundreds of times a minute. The watch reads your pulse.

2021 · 60 Minutes

Yuval Noah Harari names the next step. “The next phase is surveillance going under our skin.”

He had already given Davos the formula. Biological knowledge, times computing power, times data, equals the ability to hack a human being.

January 2026

Now the waves themselves. A president brags about a secret weapon he calls the Discombobulator. A senior official says he is probably blending several tools under one name.

That same month, reports say the Pentagon spent a year testing a backpack-sized device that emits pulsed radio waves, hoping to explain Havana Syndrome. That question is still open. The appetite is on the record.

So ask it straight. In a world where a nervous system can be hijacked, is free will dead?

Then ask the follow-up. Was it already dead in 1879?

Circa 1915 · Moscow

A century before anyone said hackable, G. I. Gurdjieff said it flatter. “Man is a machine.” Things happen to him, and he calls it doing.

He also taught a way out, in small closed groups. It began with one act. Remember yourself.

II

The Veto

Chapter II of V550 milliseconds

1983 · San Francisco

Benjamin Libet wires volunteers to an EEG and asks them to flick a wrist whenever the urge comes.

The brain starts ramping up about 550 milliseconds before the hand moves. The feeling of deciding arrives near 200.

The headlines wrote themselves. The brain decides, you find out later, free will is dead.

Libet never signed on. He pointed at what was left on the clock, which was time to cancel. The nickname that stuck was free won’t.

2012 · Saclay, France

Aaron Schurger rereads the ramp. Brain activity drifts up and down all day, like weather. Told to move whenever, people tend to move as the drift nears a threshold.

Average a few hundred of those moments and the weather looks like a decision made in advance.

2016 · Berlin

A team at the Charité stages a duel. A computer reads the ramp live and flashes a stop light when it sees a move coming. People could still cancel after the ramp began.

The door shut about 200 milliseconds before the move. They named it the point of no return.

So the will keeps hours. It is open while an impulse is still climbing, and it closes a fifth of a second before the body commits.

Free will only exists in the present moment.

Will lives in the veto.

The old schools had words for guarding that window.

καιρός, kairos, the opening that can be seized.νῆψις, nepsis, the desert monks’ sober watch.προσοχή, prosoche, the Stoics’ attention to oneself.

They were training the exact muscle the hackers go after.

III

The Lodge

Chapter III of VParallel systems

The law runs behind. A remedy needs a harm somebody has already noticed, and the tech ships first. So this piece keeps its eyes on the tech.

The hope will come through parallel systems.

Secret societies, in a time where secrets don’t exist.

That only sounds impossible until you see where the secret went. It moved into places the dragnet cannot read. Five of them are already built.

  1. I
    The mathA zero-knowledge proof lets you prove one fact and reveal nothing else. Old enough to drink. A member. Good for the money. Cryptographers have had the idea since 1985. It is the lodge password rebuilt in arithmetic. The doorkeeper learns that you belong, and that is all he learns.
  2. II
    Your own machineA model that runs on hardware you own does its thinking about you at home. Point it outward and it strips trackers, reads the fine print and feeds the profilers junk. AI will be necessary to guard you against AI. Brunton and Nissenbaum wrote the field manual in 2015 and called it Obfuscation.
  3. III
    Parallel pipesMesh radios pass a message from device to device with no tower in the loop. Open relay protocols carry posts with no company at the center to lean on. The circle owns its own wire.
  4. IV
    The meaningA sensor captures the signal. The circle holds the context. Thieves’ cant, hobo signs, Polari, every scene’s slang. A dense shared language works like a cipher, and the model on the other end can only guess.
  5. V
    The roomPhones in a box. Faces around a table. Cryptographers already made a ritual of it. At a key-signing party, strangers meet in person, check each other’s papers and vouch with a signature. That is an initiation.

The lineage is long. Scholars in the 1600s ran a Republic of Letters on private mail. Soviet readers retyped banned books through carbon paper, passed the copies hand to hand, and called it samizdat. In 1993 Eric Hughes gave the cypherpunks their line. “Cypherpunks write code.”

Each one was a parallel system, and each one guarded the same thing. A room where a person could still hear their own veto.

IV

Something Getting Closer

Chapter IV of VOn approach

Look at the dot on the long orbit. It has been closing since the first line you read.

It moved while you scrolled, and it moved while you stopped.

You probably took it for the machine. The sensors, the feed and the waves, all arriving at once.

That was a fair guess.

It has a name, and the name is old. Νέμεσις.

Nemesis, from nemein, to deal out what is due. The Greeks also called her Adrasteia, the one nobody outruns.

1984

Astronomers borrowed her name for a dark companion star they suspected was circling the Sun and sending comets our way every 26 million years. Sky surveys have found nothing there.

She has always been easier to feel than to see.

Her beat is hubris, ὕβρις. She comes for mortals who decide they are gods.

The man who called us hackable animals titled his book about upgrading the species Homo Deus. Read it as a forecast if you like. A Greek would hear a summons.

Second century · Hadrian’s court

Mesomedes, court musician to the emperor Hadrian, wrote her a hymn. She tips the scales of a life. A wheel turns under her. She checks the swagger of mortals with a bridle that does not give.

A Renaissance emblem adds a measuring rod. His hymn is one of four that survive with their musical notation, so her tune is still on the page.

Now look at your own line. Two gold rails went up when you learned the veto. A limit and a rein.

A measuring rod and a bridle. πῆχυς. χαλινός.

She is coming for the overreach, and she is carrying the two tools you already picked up.

Dread, meet your ally.

Every room in the lodge runs one of her errands. Measure. Restraint. What is due, dealt out.

The last chapter opens when you stop.

Hold still for five seconds. That is twenty-five veto windows laid end to end.

Waiting for stillness.

V

Grace

Chapter V of VDaylight

You stopped. That was the whole lesson, done with a thumb.

Now the second half of the sentence.

Free will only exists in the present moment, and we are still subject to the consequences of others’ actions.

Everyone you meet today runs a nervous system tuned by forces they never chose. The lamp, the ad, the lead, the feed. Some of them have never once found the window.

Their choices land on you anyway, and yours land on them.

So the veto has a second job. Your first read of another person is usually a verdict, built from data with no context. Veto that one too.

Hold the window open for them a beat longer than they have learned to hold it for themselves. That is grace, and it runs on a clock like everything else here.

Find grace. Know grace. Be grace. But don’t be gullible.

She arrives. The bridle is in your hand.

Will lives in the veto.

Michael Wilhelm · November 2026
