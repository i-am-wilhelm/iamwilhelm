Seen · No. 14 · A scrolly on light, bubbles, sand and the edge of the table

Living Vanitas

For four hundred years painters have set a table with a skull, a bubble and an hourglass to say one thing: this will pass. Here is how that table is built, and what it looks like set with the things on your desk.

A marked point on the forehead shows its angle and its brightness: 1 when it faces the light, one half at 60 degrees, 0 at 90 degrees.

I · The window

Every table of this kind starts with one decision: where the window is. The painters most often set it high and to the left, just outside the frame.

A surface that faces the light is bright. Turn it away and it dims by the cosine of the angle. That one rule models the whole skull. Slide the window.

Johann Lambert wrote the rule down in 1760. The painters had worked it out by looking, more than a century earlier.

A slider ages the bubble. A new bubble's film is about 600 nanometers thick at the top and thicker at the bottom, and shows bands of green and pink. As it drains, the bands slide downward. When the top is thinner than about 50 nanometers it reflects no colour and looks black. At the end of the slider the bubble bursts. A scale beside it shows which colour each thickness gives.

II · The bubble

The oldest painting of this kind that anyone knows floats a soap bubble over a skull. Jacques de Gheyn painted it in 1603.

The Latin tag is homo bulla: man is a bubble. The Roman writer Varro was already calling it an old saying two thousand years ago.

A bubble's colours are a ruler. The film is about as thick as a wave of light is long, and each thickness keeps some colours and cancels others. Slide the age and the film drains.

When the film is too thin for any colour, it goes black. Then it is gone.

A slider runs an hour. The sand in the hourglass falls at a steady pace, so half of it is left at 30 minutes. The candle shortens at a steady pace too, and at 60 minutes it is out, with a thread of smoke. On the chart the line for sand is straight. The line for water draining from a jar is curved: three quarters of the water is gone at 30 minutes, and the last of it takes the longest.

III · The glass

Time gets two objects on the table: an hourglass and a flame. Both are honest clocks.

Sand has an odd property. It runs through the neck at the same pace whether the top bulb is full or nearly empty. Water draining from a jar slows as its level falls.

So the line for sand is straight. Slide the minutes and watch both.

When the flame goes out, the painters keep the smoke. In Pieter Claesz's small panel of 1628 the wick of the lamp is still guttering.

A slider pushes the plate out over the edge of the table, from fully on the table to 70 percent off it. A dot marks the middle of the plate. While the dot is over the table the plate holds. Once more than half the plate is past the edge, the dot is over empty air and the plate tips. A strip of lemon peel hangs from the plate in a spiral.

IV · The edge

Look at where the table ends. In painting after painting a plate, a knife or a strip of lemon peel hangs out over the edge, toward you.

It does two jobs. It makes the table seem to carry on into the room where you are standing. And it looks as if it could fall.

The peel is a spiral, drawn here with a cosine. Willem Heda's banquet of 1635 hangs one over the front of the table.

A plate holds until its middle passes the edge. Slide it out and find the point.

The table · 1640 to today

1640

A slider swaps five objects, one at a time. The hourglass becomes a phone at 3 percent battery. The bubble becomes a red notification badge. The lemon peel over the edge becomes a charging cable that hangs in a curve called a catenary. The coins become a lanyard and name badge. The tipped glass becomes a tipped paper cup. The skull, the books and the smoking candle stay as they are.

V · The table

Put it together. One window. A skull on a book. A bubble, an hourglass, a tipped glass, a few coins, a peel over the edge. That is the grammar.

Now change the nouns. Slide from 1640 to today.

The hourglass becomes a phone at 3%. The bubble becomes a notification. The peel becomes a charging cable, which hangs in a curve with an equation of its own.

The skull and the smoke stay. They needed no translation.

That is the column: the same table, set again each month with what is on the desk.

This will pass.
Look closely anyway.

The painters set themselves a hard problem: say that nothing lasts, using objects that are a pleasure to look at. Four centuries on, their answer still reads.

Next time you meet one of these tables in a gallery, find the window first. Then find what hangs over the edge.
