Nobody Sees the Same Sea

A red fish is only red near the surface. A cuttlefish matches colors it cannot see. One deep-sea fish hunts with a light nobody else knows is on.

I · The water eats the colors

Sunlight is every color at once. Water takes the colors back one at a time. Watch the striped fish as we sink.

Red goes first. About thirteen meters down, in the clearest water, one part in a hundred of the red light is left. A red fish looks gray-brown, even at noon.

Orange and yellow go next. Blue travels deepest. The open sea looks blue because blue is the light still on its way down.

Each meter of water takes the same share of what is left. That rule is called exponential decay, and it explains why the dark arrives so fast. Below about 200 meters there is too little light for plants. Below 1,000 meters there is none.

II · The colorblind master of color

A cuttlefish can copy sand, pebbles, even a checkerboard. Vision tests say it cannot see color at all.

Its skin holds up to 200 tiny bags of pigment in every square millimeter: yellow, red and brown. Muscles pull each bag open like a tent. Open bag, big dot. Closed bag, pale skin.

That makes the animal a living newspaper photo, redrawn in under a second. Pick a new floor with the buttons and watch it catch up.

How it gets the colors right is still an open question. One clue: scientists have found the light-catching molecules that eyes use in cuttlefish skin too.

III · Twelve kinds of color sensor

Your eye has three kinds of color sensor. Each one answers best to one color and less to its neighbors. Your brain compares the three and builds every color you know.

A mantis shrimp has twelve, reaching from ultraviolet to deep red. For years people assumed it saw a rainbow far richer than ours.

Then scientists tested it. People can tell two colors apart when they differ by 1 to 4 nanometers. The shrimp needed 12 to 25. Twelve sensors, and worse at the job.

The leading idea: it skips the comparing. Each sensor answers yes or no, and the pattern of answers names the color at once. Speed matters to an animal that hunts with a punch.

IV · One eye, two worlds

Light bends when it crosses from air into water. A straw in a glass looks broken for the same reason.

From below, the whole sky squeezes into one bright circle overhead. A fish looking up sees the bug higher than it really is.

The four-eyed fish has an answer. It floats with its eyes half out of the water. Each eye has two pupils: one above the waterline to watch for birds, one below for bigger fish. Two eyes doing the work of four.

V · Bring your own light

Two hundred meters down, the only sunlight is a faint blue glow from above. Anything swimming overhead shows up as a shadow.

So the hatchetfish lights its own belly to match the glow, and its shadow disappears. Try it with the slider.

Making light is normal down here. Scientists counted more than 350,000 animals in deep-sea video off California. Three out of four belonged to kinds that can glow.

Nearly all of that light is blue-green, the color that travels farthest. Remember the red the water ate? Most animals down here cannot see red at all.

One fish uses that. The stoplight loosejaw, a kind of dragonfish, makes its own red light and can see it. It hunts with a flashlight nobody else knows is on.

Every animal lives in the sea its own eyes can make. Yours is one version.

Next time you stand at a tank, ask what the animal inside is seeing. It may be looking at a different room.
