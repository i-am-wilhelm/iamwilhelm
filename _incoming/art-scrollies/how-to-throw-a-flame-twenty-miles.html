How to Throw a Flame Twenty Miles

A lamp shines in every direction, and most of that light never reaches a ship. In 1823 a French engineer cut a lens into rings, and one flame could be seen from twenty miles out.

I · The flame

A lamp throws light in every direction: up at the clouds, down at the rocks, back at the land. A ship gets the thin slice that happens to point its way.

And light thins as it travels. Twice as far away, the same light covers four times the area, so it looks a quarter as bright. Slide the ship out.

Before 1823, lighthouses put curved mirrors behind their lamps. The mirrors sent back only about half of what struck them.

II · The bend

Glass bends light. The rule is the one that squeezed the whole sky into a fish's window in the sea piece.

Shape the glass into a lens and put the lamp at one exact spot, called the focus. Every ray that hits the lens leaves it side by side with the others. Slide the lamp and find the spot.

People had tried solid lenses. A lens big enough for a lighthouse is a boulder of glass, and thick glass swallows much of the light it is meant to pass.

A slider sets the number of rings from 1 to 12. With one ring the second lens is the solid dome. With twelve it is a thin plate with a sawtooth face that uses about a sixth of the glass. Above and below the plate, small prisms catch steep rays from the lamp and turn them level.

III · The rings

Augustin Fresnel, a French engineer, saw that only the curved face of a lens does the bending. The glass behind it is along for the ride.

So he sliced the curve into rings and slid each ring back toward the flat side. The slopes stay the same and most of the glass is gone. Slide the rings.

Past the edge of the lens he added prisms. Light that meets the inside of a glass face at a steep enough slant cannot get out, and bounces. The prisms catch stray rays and turn them into the beam.

His first lighthouse lens was lit on July 25, 1823, at Cordouan in France. It could be seen from more than twenty miles out.

At 67 meters, the height of its lamp on the cliff, it is about 29 kilometers. A boat with a lookout 5 meters up can see the light from 8 kilometers farther still. IV · The reach

A perfect beam still meets one limit. The sea curves away, and light goes straight.

The higher the lamp, the farther its horizon. At the mouth of the Columbia River, the Cape Disappointment tower is 16 meters tall. The cliff under it lifts the lamp to 67.

Slide the height. Four times the height buys twice the distance.

Height cannot see around a headland. Ships coming from the north could not see the Cape Disappointment light, so in 1898 a second light was built at North Head.

Four buttons choose a light. Cape Disappointment sends one white beam and one red beam from opposite sides of a lens that turns once in 30 seconds, so a sailor sees white, then red 15 seconds later. North Head sends two white beams a quarter turn apart: two flashes seven and a half seconds apart, every 30 seconds. Cordouan's first lens had eight panels and turned once in eight minutes: one flash a minute. The Cape Mendocino lens, now in Astoria, has sixteen panels on the same eight-minute turn: one flash every 30 seconds. The animation runs faster than life.

V · The signature

At night every light is a dot. So each one is given a rhythm, and sailors tell them apart by counting.

The rhythm comes from the lens. Each panel throws its own beam, the whole lens turns, and every beam that sweeps past is a flash.

Cape Disappointment shows white, then red, in a 30-second cycle. North Head shows two white flashes every 30 seconds. Pick a light and count.

Fresnel planned it that way from the start: eight panels, one flash a minute.

Sixteen panels on the same eight-minute turn double the count. That is the Cape Mendocino lens, lit in 1868: one flash every thirty seconds. It stands in Astoria now.

One flame,
twenty miles.

Bend it, fold the glass, lift it high and give it a rhythm. The flame is as small as it ever was. All the gain is geometry.

Next time you stand in front of a lighthouse lens, count its rings. Each one is a slice of a lens too heavy to build.
