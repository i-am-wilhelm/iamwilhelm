Seen · No. 10 · A scrolly on dust, acid and a double meaning

The Sleep of Reason

In 1799 Francisco Goya needed gray, and an etched line can only print black. So he let dust fall on a sheet of copper, let acid keep the time, and wrote four words that still mean two things.

The drawing on the plate is a mirror image: the desk sits on the right. Beside the plate, five small squares are filled with parallel lines that sit closer and closer together, and from a distance they read as five grays. At the end the plate turns over and becomes a print in black line on cream paper, with the desk now on the left.

I · The line

An etching starts with a copper plate coated in wax. The artist scratches a drawing through the wax with a needle. Acid bites the bare lines into the metal.

Ink sits in the bitten lines and prints black. Everything else prints white. A line is all or nothing.

To get gray, you pack lines side by side. Closer lines, darker gray. It works, and it always looks like lines.

The picture Goya had in mind needed more: flat, soft tone, the kind a brush gives. Copper could give him that, with the help of some dust.

II · The dust

The trick is called aquatint. Powdered tree resin is blown into the air inside a box and left to settle on the plate like fine snow.

Heat melts every grain onto the copper. Acid cannot reach the metal under a grain. Slide the dust and watch how much of the plate gets covered.

Nobody places the grains. They land at random, and the total still obeys a rule: more dust means more cover, with a little less gained each time.

Then the plate goes into the acid. On paper each grain prints as a white speck, and the bitten copper between the grains prints dark. The specks are too small to pick out. Together they read as gray.

A slider sets the minutes. At zero the picture is only line. The four words on the desk, the man's sleeves and the animals' eyes stay white throughout. The man's coat and the floor stop darkening after about one minute, the desk and the wall behind after about six, and the night sky keeps going to thirty. A small chart shows the curve of tone against time with a dot where each part stopped.

III · The clock

How dark? That depends on time. A bath of a minute prints pale. Half an hour prints close to black.

So the work is done with varnish and a clock. Before the first bath, everything that should stay white gets painted over. That includes the four words on the desk.

After each bath, the parts that are dark enough get varnished too, and the plate goes back in. Each tone in the picture is a different length of time.

Drag the clock. Every part darkens together until its varnish goes on. Then it stops, and the rest keep going.

The words had to be painted backwards, the way Blake wrote his. A plate is always the mirror of its print.

IV · The monsters

A man has fallen asleep at his desk, among his drawing tools. Behind him the air fills with owls and bats.

They rise out of the darkest tone on the plate, the one that sat longest in the acid.

Use the slider. The more soundly reason sleeps, the more of them come.

This is plate 43 of the 80 prints Goya published in 1799 as Los Caprichos. He first thought of putting it at the front of the book.

Read sueño as

Two buttons choose the reading. With Sleep, the caption under the print reads The sleep of reason produces monsters, and the owls and bats fly in from the dark corner and settle around the man. With Dream, the caption reads The dream of reason produces monsters, and the same creatures start small at his head and fly outward.

V · The word

The desk says El sueño de la razon produce monstruos. In Spanish, sueño means sleep. It also means dream.

Read it as sleep, and the monsters arrive when thinking stops. Choose Sleep and watch where they come from.

Read it as dream, and the monsters are what thinking makes when nothing checks it. Choose Dream.

Goya's own caption takes neither side. Imagination left alone by reason makes impossible monsters, he wrote. Joined with reason, it is the mother of the arts.

A picture that holds two readings lets each viewer finish it. Satire has leaned on that ever since.

The sleep,
or the dream.

Dust, acid and a clock made the grays. One word made the meaning double.

Next time a picture seems to say two things at once, ask whether that was the plan.
