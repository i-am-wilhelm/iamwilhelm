I · Three strata

Erwin Panofsky, 1939, Studies in Iconology. Every picture has three layers, and most people stop at the first. Start there: thirteen figures, one long table, bread, cups. That is everything the eye reports.

Stratum two needs culture. If you grew up anywhere near a church you said it before the halo appeared: the Last Supper. The same thirteen shapes, plus a story you were handed.

Stratum three asks why this picture, this way, this century. Why the lines of the room converge on one head. Why it went on a dining-hall wall so that monks ate beneath it. Panofsky called this layer iconology and treated it as the real subject of art history.

Irving Lavin wrote that Panofsky's genius was his insistence on meaning in places where no one suspected there was any. Keep that sentence. It is the whole blog.

II · The meme

Now the same three strata on a picture you actually look at. Stratum one: a cat sitting on a laptop.

Stratum two: an image macro. You know the dialect: bold white type with a black outline, setup on top, the picture is the punchline. You know a cat on a keyboard is the universal interrupter. None of that is in the pixels. It is in you.

Stratum three: a decade of people joking, together, about never getting to the work. The format carried a mood. Historians will read it the way Panofsky read altarpieces.

Tap the picture to see it the way someone without the codes sees it. That is stratum one, alone.

III · Disguised symbolism

Panofsky's second big idea, from Early Netherlandish Painting (1953): in fifteenth-century Flemish pictures, ordinary household objects are also words. He called it disguised symbolism.

His example was Robert Campin's Mérode Altarpiece. A pot of lilies sits on a table, perfectly at ease. Panofsky's point: without hundreds of other Annunciations to compare it to, nobody could guess from this one that the lilies mean anything at all.

Lilies: purity. The candle, just snuffed, smoke still rising: the natural light yielding to the divine one entering the room. The basin and towel: cleansing. A merchant's parlour, and every object in it is praying.

The whole room is an Annunciation hiding as a still life. The viewer who knows, knows. The viewer who doesn't gets a very good painting of a table.

IV · Build for three

This is the operating rule for every image on this site. Stratum one: clean and beautiful with no key at all. Stratum two: the cultured reader catches the quotation. Stratum three: a private signature only the initiated recognize.

Peter Burke's warning belongs here. The lazy version of Panofsky is a matching game, symbol to meaning. The third layer is where the work is. Ask why this sign, here, for these people, now.

Meaning in places where nobody suspected there was any.

Form, convention, meaning. Panofsky built the ladder for Renaissance altarpieces; it climbs just as well on an image macro, a logo, a campaign poster, a scroll page. The first rung is free. The second you earn by paying attention to a culture. The third is the one worth writing about, and the one this blog is for.

Every picture here is built to hold all three.
