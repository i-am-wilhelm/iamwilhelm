The Mask Is the Method

When a thing cannot be said, it gets drawn as something else. Four moves that have carried pictures past the censor since 1799, and the reason they work.

Paris, 1831 · the king and the pear
shape = (1 − s) · head + s · pearevery point slides from one outline to the other
likeness = 1 − sthe law needs a line. the drawing has none
0%

A slider changes a drawn head into a pear. At 0 it is a round-jawed head with a tuft of hair. At 33 percent the top has narrowed. At 67 percent the face is a few faint marks and the tuft is nearly a stem. At 100 it is a pear with a stem and a leaf. A mark at 50 percent shows where a court might draw its line. Four small copies below show the four stages on one sheet.

I · The line

Paris, November 14, 1831. Charles Philipon, editor of a satirical weekly, is on trial for a cartoon of the king.

His defense is a drawing. Four heads on one sheet: the first is the king, the last is a pear, and no step between them is large. Which one, he asks the court, is the crime? Slide it.

He was fined and jailed all the same. The pear went free. Soon it was being drawn on walls across Paris.

The first move: a likeness is a slider. A law has to mark a line somewhere along it, and the artist can always stand one step past the line.

Madrid, 1799 · nobody knows anybody
mask(a) = mask(b) = mask(c)many faces in, one face out
odds it is you = 1 ÷ faces that fita function with no way back
1

A slider sets how many people the masked figure could be, from 1 to 12. Each appears as a small figure in a different hat with a line to the mask. With one, the picture is a portrait of that person. With twelve, the odds that it shows any one of them are 1 in 12.

II · The mask

Goya, 1799. Plate 6 of his Caprichos shows masked figures in costume over the caption Nadie se conoce. Nobody knows anybody.

A mask is a function with no way back. Put any face in and the same face comes out. From outside, nobody can work out who went in.

So a masked figure accuses a type, and each viewer decides who fits. Slide the number of faces that fit and watch the odds that it means you.

The commentary thought to be Goya's own stays general: the world is a masquerade. Another from his time points at a general. One picture, two keys.

Warsaw, 1950s on · one thing for another
picture = scene − one thing + anotherthe viewer supplies the reason
signal ≈ 1 ÷ number of swapsmore surprises, less message
0

A slider adds swaps to a poster, from none to four. With none it is a plain figure in a suit. With one, the head is an open birdcage. With two, the tie is also a fish. With three, the pocket square is a paper boat. With four, the flat background is a brick wall. One swap reads at a glance. Four compete with each other.

III · The swap

Warsaw, from the 1950s. The state pays for film and theatre posters, and its censors check them.

The designers answer with metaphor. Take a plain picture, remove one thing, put another in its place. The poster never states what it means. The viewer works it out on the walk home.

One swap is enough. Slide the number of swaps and watch the picture get louder and say less.

Henryk Tomaszewski, Jan Lenica, Roman Cieślewicz and others made the street their gallery. In 1968 Warsaw opened the world's first poster museum to keep the work.

Rafał Olbiński took the method to New York in 1981. He calls it poetic surrealism. His posters are in copyright, so they are linked below and none is redrawn here.

Moscow, c. 1918 · same face, new ground
meaning = figure × groundthe face stays put. the reading moves
1 figure × 3 grounds = 3 readingsthe viewer does the multiplying
Ground

Three buttons change the background behind one cut-out figure with a blank face. At a party, under bunting and balloons, the figure reads as left out. In a flood, with water to the waist and rain falling, the figure reads as resigned. In a queue of grey figures, it reads as patient. The cut-out is identical each time.

IV · The cut

Around 1918 the Russian filmmaker Lev Kuleshov spliced one shot of an actor's blank face between a bowl of soup, a coffin and a woman on a couch. By the usual account, audiences saw hunger, grief and desire.

The face never changed. The meaning came from what stood next to it. Pick a ground and read one figure three ways.

Collage runs on this. Since 2016 Christopher Spencer, who works as Cold War Steve, has pasted one weary soap-opera character into scene after scene of British public life. The bystander never changes. The scene carries the joke.

His collages are built from press photographs of real people, so they are described here and linked below.

The gap · getting it and proving it
gap = readers who get it − what a censor can provethe four moves all widen this
screen + markerone part hides it. one part tells you it is there
plain

A slider moves from saying a thing plainly to hiding it completely. Said plainly, nearly all readers get it and the censor can prove it. In the middle, nearly all readers still get it and the censor can prove almost nothing: the gap is widest, about 90 points. Hidden completely, nobody gets it. The curves are a sketch of the idea and were not measured.

V · The gap

All four moves do one job. They open a gap between two readers: the public, who only has to get it, and the censor, who has to prove it.

Say it plainly and both get it. Bury it too deep and neither does. Slide the dial and find the range where the public still reads it and the charge will not stick.

To ban the picture, the censor must say out loud what it means. That is usually the last thing a censor wants said.

Lev Loseff wrote this way in Soviet Leningrad before he studied it. He named the two parts: a screen that hides the message from the censor, and a marker that tells the reader it is there.

The curves on this chart are a sketch of the idea. Nobody has measured them.

The mask does the hiding.
You do the reading.

A coded picture is left unfinished on purpose. The last step is the viewer's, and that step cannot be seized, fined or jailed.

Next time a poster shows you one thing where another should be, ask what it is trusting you to finish.
