Redon's Turn

For about twenty years Odilon Redon worked in black: charcoal, and prints the color of soot. Then, in his fifties, he opened a box of pastels.

I · The black

Odilon Redon was born in Bordeaux in 1840. From 1871 into the 1890s nearly everything he made was black: charcoal drawings and prints. He called them his noirs.

Charcoal is burnt wood. Drag it across paper and the paper's bumps scrape off dust. Press lightly and only the peaks catch. Press hard and the valleys fill too.

Slide the pressure. One stick and one sheet give every gray from mist to pitch.

His black pictures are full of creatures: a spider with a grin, an eye that floats like a balloon.

II · The light

In a charcoal drawing, the brightest thing is the paper. Light is made by leaving it bare, or by lifting the black back off.

This is his eye-balloon, from a print of 1882: a giant eye drifting up over dark water, with a small gondola hanging beneath.

The glow around it follows a bell curve: brightest at the center, fading fast, never quite reaching zero. Slide the glow.

One historian says Redon wanted his pictures to show light winning over darkness.

III · The turn

In the 1890s Redon took up pastel and oil paint, and they became his favorite materials. After 1900 he made no more noirs.

Pastel is close kin to charcoal: a stick of dust dragged over rough paper. His hand already knew the motion. The dust was new.

Slide the color. The picture underneath stays the same. Each gray gives way to the hue it was standing in for.

A slider sets a number k from 2 to 9. Beside the bouquet one large flower is traced slowly as a single line. With k at 3 it has three petals, at 4 it has eight, at 5 it has five. All fifteen flowers in the vase change with it.

IV · The flowers

From then on he drew flowers again and again: bouquets in vases, some real and some invented.

A flower is a curve that keeps coming home. Let the distance from the center rise and fall as you go around, and petals appear. Slide k.

Odd values of k give k petals. Even values give twice as many. Nobody expects that the first time.

He set opposite colors side by side, yellow against violet and red against green, so that each makes the other glow.

A slider marked Black runs from none to all. With none, the darkest thing in the picture is a middle gray and a readout gives the range from lightest to darkest as about 0.5. With all of it, the ground is nearly black, the vase casts a shadow, and the range is about 0.85.

V · What he kept

The easy story is that he left black behind. Take the black out of the bouquet and see what happens.

The flowers are still bright, and the picture goes flat. The darks were holding the lights up.

A picture's reach is the distance from its lightest light to its darkest dark. Twenty years of charcoal had taught him the bottom of that scale.

In the sea piece of this series, colors vanish as you sink and return as you rise. Redon's working life can be read the same way, with one addition. He brought the dark back up with him.

Down into black,
and back with color.

The hand that had learned every gray picked up color and already knew where the shadows go.

Next time a bright picture holds your eye, look for the dark that is doing the work.
