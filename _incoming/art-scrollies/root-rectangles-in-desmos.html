I · The square and its diagonal

Start with a square, side one. Its diagonal is √2. Swing that diagonal down to the baseline and you have a rectangle one by √2: the root-two rectangle. Paper sizes still use it.

Do it again. The diagonal of the root-two rectangle is √3. Swing it: root-three. Again: root-four, which is two squares. Again: root-five. Jay Hambidge, 1920, called these dynamic rectangles, because each one is made from the one before by a motion.

His claim: Greek vases and temples were laid out on these proportions, and rectangles past root-five almost never appear in human design. Historians have doubted the Greeks did it on purpose since 1921. That argument is beside the point here.

The point is that the whole system is a square, a diagonal, and an arc. Three functions. A generation of kids on Desmos figured out you can draw anything with that.

II · The amphora

Stand the root-two rectangle up. Draw a diagonal, then drop a perpendicular onto it from the opposite corner: that line is the reciprocal. Where they cross is the rectangle's eye. Do it from all four corners and you have four eyes.

The eyes of a root-two rectangle sit at thirds: one third up, two thirds up, one third across, two thirds across. Hambidge's vase chapters are mostly this. Find the eyes, hang the pot on them.

So the belly turns at the lower third. The neck sits exactly on the inner vertical lines. The handles spring from the upper eyes and land on the lip. Six points, one smooth curve through them, mirrored.

That is a pelike. It is also a table of six coordinates and a spline, and the list on the left is the entire file.

III · The armature

Lay the rectangle down. Both diagonals, all four reciprocals: the armature. Painters have pinned compositions to this skeleton since the Baroque, whether or not they knew the name.

The Baroque diagonal runs lower-left to upper-right; to a left-to-right reader it feels like ascent. The other one, the sinister diagonal, feels like a fall or a shove. Put the action on one and the light on the other.

Watch three figures find the eyes. Nothing is centered. Everything holds. That is what dynamic symmetry means: balance that moves.

Cartier-Bresson shot on this grid. Orozco painted the New School murals on it. It is a diagonal and its perpendicular.

IV · Growth

Hambidge's last claim was the biggest: that these proportions are the symmetry of growth itself. His evidence was the whirling-square rectangle, φ, 1.618, and the spiral it throws off when you keep cutting squares away.

Here is the same arithmetic in a sunflower. Each seed turns 137.5° from the last and steps out as the square root of its number. Two functions. The spiral arms were never drawn; they appear.

The same arithmetic grows a sunflower and fills a vase.
