Type Is an Image

You stopped seeing letters the day you learned to read. Every one is still a drawing: a pen stroke, cast in metal, traced in curves, filled in with squares of gray.

I · The pen

Every letter on this screen began as a pen stroke. The pen had a flat tip, wide one way and thin the other, and the writer held it at one steady angle.

Follow the pen around the o. Nobody chose where the line gets thick. The angle of the tip chose it.

Turn the pen with the slider. The thin spots move, and the letter changes character. Much of the difference between an old typeface and a modern one comes down to this one angle.

Now make the strokes straight and steep, and pack them close. German scribes wrote Bibles this way in the 1400s. Each short upright stroke is called a minim.

Here is the word minimum: fifteen minims in a row. It was handsome and hard to read. Readers needed help finding the i, and that is how the i got its dot.

On the punch the R is raised and backwards. Struck into copper, it leaves a sunken R that reads forwards. Metal cast in that hollow gives a raised, backwards R on a piece of type. Inked and pressed, the type prints a forwards R on paper. At the end, a grid of 290 small squares fills in, one for every different character in the font Gutenberg used for his Bible.

II · The metal

In Mainz, in the early 1450s, Johannes Gutenberg set out to make that handwriting repeatable. His Bible has 42 lines to a page. The first copies were ready by 1454 or 1455.

The classic method takes three flips. First, carve the letter backwards on the end of a steel bar. That is the punch.

Strike the punch into soft copper. The dent reads the right way round. That is the matrix.

Pour hot metal into the dent: lead, tin and antimony. Out comes a piece of type, backwards again. Ink it, press it, and the page reads forwards.

The Bible font needed about 290 different characters: the alphabet, plus the joined letters and shortcuts that scribes used. Printers went on cutting punches by hand for about four hundred years.

How Gutenberg cast his own first type is still argued over. In 2001 two researchers at Princeton measured his printed letters and found more small differences than one mould should give.

III · The outline

Metal type lasted about five centuries. Then the letter moved into the computer, and it had to become numbers.

A font file stores each letter as a short list of points. Between the points, one small formula draws the curve. Slide t from 0 to 1 and watch it draw.

Two of the points sit on the letter. The third pulls the curve toward itself and never touches it.

This o is sixteen of those curves. That is 32 points, or 64 numbers. The file holds no picture of the letter at all.

Because the letter is a formula, it can be the size of an ant or the side of a building, and the edge stays clean.

The grid starts at 8 squares by 8. Two buttons switch between filling each square black or white, which gives the letter a stair-step edge, and filling it with shades of gray, which looks smooth from a distance. The grid then grows finer, up to 64 by 64, and the squares become too small to notice. At the end, small copies of both versions sit side by side: the gray one reads as a smooth letter and the black and white one stays jagged.

IV · The pixels

A screen cannot draw a curve. It has a grid of tiny squares, and each square can show only one shade.

So the computer asks every square one question: how much of you does the letter cover? All of it, black. None of it, white. Part of it, gray.

Try it both ways with the buttons. With black and white only, the edge turns into stairs. With grays, your eye smooths the stairs back into a curve.

Add more squares and the steps get too small to see. The words you are reading now are built this way. Each letter is a little mosaic of grays.

A newspaper photo, a cuttlefish and a letter on a phone share one trick: small spots, blended by your eye.

You are not reading these words one letter at a time. Your eye lands on a line, takes a small bite, and jumps ahead. A good page makes that easy. The strokes and the gaps settle into an even gray. The lines sit far enough apart to find the next one, and near enough to belong together. When it works, you stop seeing the page at all.

V · The texture

Now step back from the letters. Before a page says anything, it has a color: an even gray, made of black strokes and white gaps.

Line spacing sets that gray. Too tight, and the lines tangle. Too loose, and they drift apart into stripes. Try the slider.

People who design pages check them by squinting until the words blur. If the gray is even, the page is working. Press Squint.

The script Gutenberg copied is called Textura. Its strokes mesh like threads in cloth. The word text comes from the Latin for a woven thing.

Two columns, forty-two lines. From across the room it is a weave. Up close it is one pen stroke, repeated.
