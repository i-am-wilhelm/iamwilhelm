A scrolly on the honeybee Arizona never had, and the one it got

Due Space

Arizona has no native honeybee. The one in the water-meter box flew up from Brazil at a hundred miles a year. She has earned her due, and she requires her space.

I · No one here by that name

Arizona has roughly thirteen hundred native bee species, and the count of native honeybees among them is zero. The genus Apis belongs to Europe, Africa and Asia. Every honeybee on this continent descends from livestock.

The first recorded hives reached Virginia by ship in 1622. Jefferson wrote that the tribes called the newcomer the white man's fly, because swarms tended to arrive a little ahead of the settlers. The bee needed 231 years to reach the Pacific.

One asterisk, fourteen million years old. In 2009 a fossil from Stewart Valley, Nevada, was identified as a true honeybee, hairy eyes and barbed stinger included. They named her Apis nearctica. The continent had a honeybee, lost her, and carried on.

Far to the south the Maya kept stingless bees for honey, long before the ships. Up here in the Sonoran Desert, hive honey was nobody's job. Pollination was everybody's.

II · The roster

One dot for every bee species native to Arizona. Most live alone, nest in bare dirt, and fly for a few weeks a year. A nine-year survey of one six-square-mile patch on the Sonora line logged 497 species, the densest bee count on record anywhere.

The specialists keep their own accounts. Cactus bees work the prickly pear and cholla. Digger bees work the palo verde. Perdita minima, the smallest bee on Earth, lives here. Now add one honey-colored dot for the import. In that survey she was 3.5 percent of the bees caught.

Sundown, and the second shift clocks in. Lesser long-nosed bats and Mexican long-tongued bats take the saguaro and the agave. Hawk moths take the datura. The yucca keeps a moth of its own and accepts no substitutes.

At dawn the white-winged dove is back on the saguaro, having timed her whole migration to the bloom. Add hummingbirds, butterflies, beetles, flies and wasps. This desert had a full roster long before the first hive box came over the pass.

III · A good idea in Brazil

In 1956 the geneticist Warwick Kerr brought African queens to São Paulo state. European honeybees had never done well in the tropics, and these thrived in heat. The plan was a calm, productive cross for Brazilian beekeepers.

In October 1957 a visiting beekeeper noticed the queen excluders on Kerr's hives, decided they were in the workers' way, and pulled them. Twenty-six swarms left for the forest. Nobody has ever recalled a bee.

They bred with every European colony they met and kept moving, sixty miles at a hop, into any hollow log. The Amazon in the seventies, Central America in 1982, Mexico by 1985. Hollywood released the disaster movie in 1978, with the bees still in South America.

October 1990, a baited trap in Hidalgo, Texas. Thirty-three years and forty-eight degrees of latitude on their own wings. Averaged out, about a degree and a half a year, a hundred miles of due north every twelve months. Three summers later, Arizona.

IV · Arizona, 1993

Summer of 1993. A swarm turns up in a state trap two miles north of Sasabe, and a colony is found already settled in a Tucson roof. The killer bee is an Arizonan.

Around Tucson, 15 percent of trapped swarms were Africanized in 1994. By 1997 it was 90. The curve is the standard shape of a takeover, drawn through those two numbers.

She won on habits. She swarms more often, she nests in anything (a water-meter box will do), and her swarms move into weak European hives, replace the queen and keep the furniture. Today every wild honeybee colony in Arizona is presumed Africanized.

V · Her due

Start with what she is owed. Sting for sting, her venom is the same as any honeybee's, and she is a little smaller than the European. She stings in defense of a nest. Away from it, on a flower, she is a bee at work.

In Brazil her line carries the whole industry. Honey went from a few thousand tons a year in the 1950s to a record 74,000 in 2024, and beekeepers there have lived with the varroa mite since 1977 without treating for it. Arizona beekeepers catch her swarms, install a calmer queen and put them to work.

She pollinates, too. In a long study of Sonoran columnar cacti, honeybees accounted for 64 to 87 percent of the daytime fruit set on saguaro and organ pipe. The immigrant pulls a real share of the day shift.

VI · Her space

Now the space. The name was earned. The Smithsonian counts about a thousand deaths since 1957, and Arizona's first came in October 1995. Disturb her nest and you take ten times the stings. The federal bee lab in Tucson sets the distance at one hundred feet, the width of a four-lane highway.

If it goes wrong, run. Straight line, face covered, into a car or a building, two football fields if there is no door. She tops out near fifteen miles an hour and follows for a quarter mile. Skip the swatting. Afterward, hire someone who removes bees for a living.

VII · Meanwhile, in Washington

December 2019. A dead hornet close to two inches long turns up near Blaine, Washington. Vespa mandarinia, the northern giant hornet, the largest hornet alive. The press went with murder hornet.

Her prey is the honeybee, and a raiding party can empty a hive in ninety minutes. Japanese honeybees grew up with her and know the counter. They mob the scout in a ball of bodies and cook her at 46 degrees Celsius. The European honeybee has no such move.

Washington did the unusual thing and won. Residents phoned in sightings, entomologists fitted live hornets with radio tags and followed them home, and four nests came out of Whatcom County in 2020 and 2021. After three clean years the species was declared eradicated in December 2024.

April 30, 2026. A state grain inspector at the Port of Vancouver finds a live yellow-legged hornet on a ship in from South Korea, the first ever on the West Coast. He photographs it and kills it. Nobody can say whether it rode in or flew aboard at the dock. Searches of the area found nothing more.

There is the difference between a stowaway and a migration. One hornet on one ship can be met at the dock. The killer bee arrived as a front the width of a continent, and nobody meets weather at the dock. You learn its habits and you give it room.

Her space.

Tally it up. Arizona's own roster runs to thirteen hundred bees, two nectar bats, a dove that keeps the saguaro's calendar, and moths for the night flowers. The honeybee is a guest among them, and a useful one. Honeybee headlines are livestock news, and the desert reads them the way a mountain reads cattle prices.

The natives ask for little. Flowers that belong here, a patch of bare dirt to nest in, a shallow dish of water with stones in it. So rest easy about the pollination. It was handled before 1622 and it is handled now.

As for the bee who came up from Brazil, she lives here, she works for free, and her terms are short. A hundred feet. Give her that and she will keep setting saguaro fruit.

Respect is a distance.
