Seven appointed seats

On April 29, 2026, the Texas Education Agency suspended the power of Beaumont ISD’s elected school board and seated seven appointed managers in its place. The same day, Commissioner Mike Morath named Sandi Massey superintendent. She had been chief of schools in Houston ISD during its own state takeover.

Beaumont has been here before. The state ran the district from 2014 to 2020 over finances and governance. This time the trigger is academic. King Middle School and Fehl-Price Elementary each logged a fifth unacceptable rating, and under a 2015 law the commissioner then has to close the schools or replace the board.

The elected trustees still hold their titles. Their votes are on ice. The state lists three conditions for handing the district back and promises no date.

The state’s case

Start where the state starts. For the 2025–26 school year, 23 of 26 rated campuses came back with a D or an F. That count comes from Unlabeled’s own tracker, built from state ratings. Preliminary spring scores put 27 percent of third graders at grade level in reading and 23 percent in math.

“This district has failed the city.”Superintendent Sandi Massey, to the board in July, per KFDM

Massey counted 19,019 teacher absence days and more than $1.3 million spent on substitutes. Her five-year plan aims to lift third-grade math from 23 percent to 60.

Her request of the teachers who met her at the first board meeting was short. She asked them to “give me a year.”

The classroom’s case

Then the changes landed. At West Brook High, one teacher describes a day that moved from 7:15 to 2:45 over to 8:30 to 4:30, with 50-minute periods stretched into 90-minute blocks. Librarians were assigned new roles as student success coaches. Unlabeled’s tracker counts 34 student-support positions cut or restructured.

People left. The district’s own count, released to Unlabeled under the Texas Public Information Act, shows 423 employee resignations and retirements as of May 26. Around then the district’s job board listed 161 open teaching positions.

Massey’s explanation is fear. Teachers resigned out of fear and uncertainty, she told 12News, and she added, “I can’t be mad at that.”

By August 22 the district reported 97 percent of teaching slots filled or in process, with 662 of 951 teachers fully certified.

In September that West Brook teacher took the public-comment microphone and gave the managers the room’s verdict.

Where is Big Lando?

His name is Greg Landry. He teaches psychology and sociology at West Brook, his students call him Big Lando, and he spent the spring and fall telling the managers what the changes looked like from his room.

October 16 was his last day in class. By October 19 he was on paid leave, under written instruction to keep quiet about it with staff, students and parents. He told 12News he believes speaking out, and coaching students on how to address a public meeting, put him there.

“I’m in teacher jail.”Greg Landry, to 12News

By his account the road to leave ran through a classroom visit from Massey and a deputy superintendent, then a meeting in the principal’s office with that deputy and West Brook’s interim principal, Richard Yoes. Keep that second name in mind.

The district had not answered 12News’s question about why when the story ran. Personnel matters can be legally confidential, and paid leave carries no finding of anything. His students answered with T-shirts.

Three days after Landry’s leave began, Tamara Richards, a local and regional president of the Association of Texas Professional Educators, stood up with a petition signed by more than 500 parents. “Thank you superintendent Massey for bringing this community together,” she said. Sixteen people spoke against the superintendent that night.

Enter the people with no label

Brooke Bellanger built Unlabeled on one rule. You meet the candidate before you learn the party.

The format is a blind forum. Candidates for local, state and federal office take questions from the room with their party labels held back. The audience votes blind. The reveal comes last. The first one ran July 16 at Avenue Axe in Port Neches, free at the door, billed as a first for Texas.

No red. No blue. Just Texans. More than 17,000 people now follow the work.

The same summer, Unlabeled pointed its rule at the school district. Listen first. Label later.

So they started filing. Public Information Act requests for resignations, vacancies, administrator pay and contractor payments. Board agendas pulled and tagged, meeting by meeting. The archive now holds 421 items, and the house rule is posted at the front door.

“Every claim should lead you back to the record.”Unlabeled, BISD Start Here

The empty chair

The Thursday this story was building toward arrived with its ending already announced. By November 13 the agenda had put the superintendent’s job into closed session. Council member Cory Crenshaw had called for new leadership. Richards had called for a vote of no confidence.

On Monday, November 16, the district said Massey would resign at the meeting. Its statement cited prayerful consideration and talks with her family. KFDM reported that an evaluation of her performance had been planned for that same night, and that its sources said the review could have gone her way.

She stayed away Thursday, and KFDM noted her empty seat. About two dozen people spoke. Then the managers accepted her resignation and a separation agreement that KFDM puts at $299,000, about a year’s salary. Her tenure ran 204 days.

The managers named Richard Yoes interim superintendent. He is a former Houston ISD official who joined Beaumont about five months ago, and he is the interim principal Landry places in that October meeting.

The labels showed up on schedule. Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa said the state had installed a leader who “sowed chaos and disrupted learning.” State Board of Education member Julie Pickren put her own on it: “Failing schools fail families.” The Texas Education Agency thanked Massey and praised her focus on students.

Massey gave her own account the next day. In a statement reported by 12News, she said she did not initiate her departure and would have welcomed the chance to keep serving. Once it was clear her time was ending, she said, “I chose to accept a peaceful transition.”

A West Brook senior named Trenden Latham asked for students, parents and teachers to have a voice in what comes next. From the dais, manager Arthur Louis answered the room.

“Parents, parents, parents. We need you more than ever.”Arthur Louis, Board of Managers, per 12News

Still on the table

One name changed. The seven appointed seats remain, and no change to the schedule or the curriculum has been announced. As of November 16, a full month after his last class, Unlabeled reported that Landry still had not been back.

On Friday the district gave West Brook a new principal, Dr. Joseph Bowser, an associate principal there for eight years.

The next superintendent is the commissioner’s appointment to make. The managers can send names, and State Rep. Christian Manuel says they hope to have someone seated before January. January matters here. Hinojosa takes office on the 19th and has pledged to replace Commissioner Morath, a change the state Senate would have to approve. Pickren notes that a new commissioner could keep these managers or seat new ones.

Unlabeled’s standing petition asks the state and the managers to explain what is changing, why, where the money goes and how success gets measured, with dated milestones for bringing back an elected board.

“We deserve a seat at the table.”Unlabeled petition, BISD: We Deserve Answers

For the transition they went further. A page called Accountability & Solutions sets out seven asks and a twelve-section resolution the managers could adopt as written. Publish the criteria for the next hire. Hold two evening listening sessions, one led by students. Seat a student advisory council by January 31. Review the new programs within 60 days. Report progress every month. Give a written status on Landry within 10 business days.

A board can act only on what its posted agenda lists, so the request is a slot on December’s.

The polls fed the asks. Twenty-four of 29 students said locked classroom doors make them feel safer, so the resolution asks the district to keep them locked. That finding favors a district decision, and it went into the file like everything else.

Next on the calendar is Unlabeled’s Jefferson County Town Hall, December 9 at 5:30 p.m. in the Neches Room in Beaumont.

The file stays

A superintendent came and went in 204 days. The file is still here. Beaumont has a source-linked record of the meetings, the decisions, the money and the open questions, built by neighbors who decided the facts go on the table before the name tags do.

Someday Beaumont elects its own school board again. Whoever runs will inherit a district and a file. The file is the part the neighbors made.
