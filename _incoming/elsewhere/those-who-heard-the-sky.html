I · The stringMeasured

Pluck a string and it sounds one note. Press it at the halfway point and the note jumps an octave. Half the length, twice the frequency.

Measured

Two thirds of the string gives the fifth, 3 to 2. Three quarters gives the fourth, 4 to 3. The intervals the ear finds sweetest are ratios of small whole numbers. Greek tradition credits the finding to Pythagoras, in the sixth century BC.

Model

Then the leap. If a string obeys number, the heavens might too. Pythagoreans taught that the planets, circling at different speeds, sound a harmony we never notice because it has been in our ears since birth. Aristotle wrote the idea down, admired its elegance, and called it untrue.

II · The scoreMeasured

1619. Johannes Kepler has Tycho Brahe's planetary observations, the best ever made by eye, and a musician's question. How much faster does a planet move at its closest to the Sun than at its farthest? For Mars, about 3 to 2. A fifth.

Composed

So he wrote each planet a part. Earth's speed changes by only 16 to 15, a semitone, and it sings mi, fa, mi. Kepler noted, drily, that this suits a world of misery and famine.

Composed

Mercury has the stretched orbit and the big range, an octave plus a minor third. Venus circles almost perfectly and barely leaves one note. In this score, eccentricity is range.

Composed

Saturn and Jupiter take the bass with a major and a minor third. Sound all six and they mostly clash. Kepler worked out that full six-part concord is so rare it may have happened only once, at the Creation.

Model

He was plain about what kind of music this is. It is for the mind, he wrote, and it makes no sound. He took it for the Creator's signature. The same book holds his third law of planetary motion, which astronomers still use.

III · The octaveModel

1926. Walter Russell, an American painter and sculptor, publishes his own periodic chart of the elements, wound into a spiral of nine octaves. He was an artist by training, and he drew his physics.

Model

His wave counts four tones in toward a bright center and the same four out again. Zero, one, two, three, four. Four, three, two, one, zero. He named the inward half gravitation and the outward half radiation.

Model

Chemistry and physics went another way, and the chart is read today as Russell's own model. It still belongs in this line. Pythagoras gave the sky ratios. Kepler gave it parts. Russell gave it octaves, all the way down to the atom.

All three drew what they believed. Then the instruments arrived.

IV · The instrumentsMeasured

1933, Holmdel, New Jersey. Karl Jansky, a Bell Labs engineer, is hunting the static that spoils transatlantic phone calls. One faint hiss keeps a strange schedule. It peaks four minutes earlier each day, in step with the stars. He has picked up the center of the Milky Way.

Measured

1960, Mount Wilson, California. Robert Leighton finds the Sun's surface rising and falling about every five minutes. The Sun rings like a struck bell, in millions of tones at once, all far below hearing.

Measured

1964, Holmdel again. Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson cannot clear a faint hiss from their horn antenna. It arrives from every direction at every hour. They evict two pigeons and scrub out the droppings. The hiss stays. It is the afterglow of the Big Bang.

Measured

1967, Cambridge. Jocelyn Bell, a graduate student, reads her telescope's output by hand on long rolls of chart paper. One smudge, which she calls scruff, keeps time. A pulse every 1.337 seconds. The team files it, half joking, as LGM-1, for little green men. It is a spinning neutron star.

Measured

2003. The Chandra X-ray telescope looks at the Perseus cluster, 250 million light-years away, and sees ripples in the hot gas around a giant black hole. Ripples in gas are sound. One wave passes every ten million years. The note is a B-flat, 57 octaves below middle C.

Measured

September 14, 2015. A wave from two merging black holes, 1.3 billion years on the road, crosses Earth. Detectors in Washington and Louisiana stretch by a thousandth of a proton's width. The signal sweeps from 35 to 250 hertz in a fifth of a second. Those are audible pitches.

V · The turn inwardMeasured

Now turn the antenna around. Nearly every cell you have holds about two meters of DNA, a twisted ladder of some six billion rungs. It makes one turn every ten and a half rungs, 3.4 nanometers per turn.

Measured

It moves. The rungs flick open and shut on their own, a motion biologists call breathing. Laser spectroscopy also finds the helix flexing at terahertz frequencies, around a trillion times a second, far above hearing.

Composed

It also reads as a score. In 1986 the geneticist Susumu Ohno turned the four bases into notes and published genes as sheet music. Genes repeat phrases the way compositions do. Here is one: TTAGGG, up to thousands of times over, at the tip of every chromosome you have.

Composed

The ladder has one rule. A pairs with T, and G pairs with C. So every melody on one strand fixes a second melody on the other. Musicians call that counterpoint. Chemists call it base pairing.

Composed

At MIT in 2019, Markus Buehler's lab built a twenty-note scale from the natural vibrations of the amino acids and played proteins as music.

Keep the two kinds of claim apart. The vibrations are physics. The melodies are a person's choice of notes.

VI · Coda

It's all a song.

Tally it up. A string in Greece. A score computed from a Danish astronomer's notebooks. A painter's spiral of octaves. Then a hiss from Sagittarius, a star that ticks every 1.337 seconds, a B-flat ten million years long, and a chirp from two black holes that arrived on a Monday in September 2015.

The first three drew what they believed the sky was doing. The rest were measurements, made mostly by people listening for something else. Jansky was chasing static. Penzias and Wilson blamed pigeons. Bell was surveying quasars.

The instruments found real vibrations at real frequencies, nearly all of them far outside human hearing, which is stranger than the harmony Pythagoras promised. Shift them by enough octaves and anyone can listen. That shift is the honest part of the job, so every sound on this page says whether it is measured, transposed, rendered or composed.

Then the same test, run on yourself. Two meters of ladder in every cell, flexing a trillion times a second, spelling the same six letters at the end of every chromosome.

Everything spins. Everything sings.
