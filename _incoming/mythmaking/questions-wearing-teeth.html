The climb

ὕψος

hypsos · height, and the Greek word for the sublime

Altitude

Everything we get told about getting better is a mountain.

Climb the ladder. Reach new heights. Peak performance. Level up. We talk about progress like it has an altitude, and for a while it does. Pick a summit, put one boot in front of the other, and the air gets thin and the view gets big.

Psychology drew it the same way. Maslow's hierarchy of needs gets taught as a pyramid: food and safety at the base, love and respect in the middle, self-actualization at the point.

The pyramid came later. A consulting psychologist drew it in 1960 for a business magazine, and it stuck, because it looks like a mountain and we already believed in mountains.

Late in his life, Maslow kept climbing past the point. In The Farther Reaches of Human Nature he wrote about people he called transcenders, whose peak moments came often enough to change how they saw everything. The holy in a kitchen. Awe at a bus stop. The whole world lit from the inside.

The Greeks had a word for those moments. Hypsos. Height. It's also their word for the sublime.

Some two thousand years ago, a writer we call Longinus wrote a whole book about it, Peri Hypsous, On the Sublime: the moments that lift you out of yourself. A line that knocks the wind out of you. A ridge view that makes your chest go quiet.

Howard Gardner, the Harvard psychologist who split intelligence into many kinds, floated one more in 1999. He called it existential intelligence: the capacity to place yourself against the biggest and smallest things there are, and to ask what a life means, what death is, where it all goes. He counted it as half an intelligence. Eight and a half, total.

Every kid has it at five. Where was I before I was born? What happens when you die? Why does the sky keep going? Then the grown-ups run out of answers, and most of us learn to quit asking.

Curiosity is what carries you up the mountain. Stop moving on it, and the sublime stops coming around.

Some of those moments are subliminal: under the threshold, too quiet to catch unless you're looking. Some are sublime and knock you flat. Both words may grow from the same Latin root, limen, the threshold. Hold onto that word. You're about to cross one.

The plunge

ἴλιγγος

ilingos · the whirl, the dizziness

Which Way Is Up

Then the ground goes.

Everybody gets one. A loss. A betrayal. A diagnosis. The night a question shows up at 3 a.m. and won't leave. You were climbing, and now you're falling, and the mountain you were so sure about is spinning past.

That's vertigo. The word is Latin, from vertere, to turn. Same root as conversion. The spin is the turn. You just can't see yet what you're turning into.

Joseph Campbell lined up hero stories from all over the world and found this moment in nearly every one. He called it the belly of the whale. The hero gets swallowed. Everybody up top figures he's gone. Down inside, the real story starts.

The threshold

ὁρίζων

horizōn · the bounding circle

The Ceiling

When you land, the sky is gone.

Look up. The horizon has closed over you like the roof of a cave, and the rock hangs down in points. Horizon is Greek, short for horizōn kyklos, the bounding circle. Most days it's a line way out there. Today it's a lid.

The birds you watched on the way up are bats now. Something in the corner growls.

Every culture builds an underworld. It's where we put whatever's too big to face.

The Greeks called its king Hades, and the old reading of the name is the Unseen. That's the whole job of the place: holding what we won't look at. The grief we never finished. The thing we did. The thing somebody did to us. The question we couldn't answer, so we buried it and set a lid on it.

Down here, every growl is a question nobody asked out loud.

You brought one thing down with you. A lamp.

Curious comes from the Latin cura, care. Same root as cure. Curiosity is care with its eyes open.

The oldest story about this lamp belongs to a girl named Psyche, which is Greek for soul. Her sisters swore the husband who only came to her in the dark was a monster, a serpent fattening her up to eat. One night she lit a lamp and held it over him. The monster was Love. Eros himself, asleep. Looking cost her plenty. It also put her on the road that ends with a mortal girl made immortal.

The lamp works three ways. Better words make better ideas. Better questions get better answers. And questioning better, slower, with the light held steady, is what pulls the mask off. Question has a quest inside it. Both come from the Latin quaerere, to seek.

Room I · Nigredo, the blackening

πρόσωπον

prosōpon · face, and the actor's mask

A Thousand Faces

They'll find out what you really are.

The walls are covered in masks. Hundreds. The face you wear at work. The one for your father. The one for the person you wanted. The one you put on when somebody asks how you're doing. Every one of them has eyes, and every set of eyes is on you.

Jung called it the persona, Latin for an actor's mask. Everybody needs one. Trouble starts when you forget you're wearing it and guard it like it's your face.

Ask it something

What if they find out? What if they find out? What if they— what if—

Ruminari, to chew the cud: the same mouthful, over and over. Watch the eyes multiply. Ask a better one.

The masks lift off the walls and drift up like ash. What glared out of them was light the whole time, and it's gold.

Here's what you were guarding: the kid who asked where he was before he was born. You put a guard on that door because what's behind it is alive. Still curious. Still five. He's been waiting for somebody to come down with a better question.

Truth · a B-value

Room II · Albedo, the whitening

κῆτος

kētos · the great fish that swallows Jonah in the Septuagint

The Man in the Whale

Who do you think you are?

Ribs arch over this room like the inside of something that swallowed you. Remember the whale? The man inside it gets a room of his own. On the far wall, something huge is breathing.

Abraham Maslow used to ask his classes which of them would write the great novel, or lead the country, or become a saint. They'd giggle and blush and squirm. Then he'd ask: “If not you, then who else?”

At a friend's suggestion, he named that squirm after Jonah, the prophet who ran from his calling and got swallowed for it. The Jonah complex: the fear of your own greatness. We're scared of our worst. We're every bit as scared of our best, because once you see it, it's yours to carry.

Ask it something

Who do you think you are? Who do you think you— who do you— who—

Asked like that, it's an accusation wearing a question's clothes, and all it can do is growl. The shape gets bigger. Ask a better one.

The eyes on the wall close. Look at the shape again. It moves when you move. It's your shadow, thrown huge across the rock by the lamp in your own hand.

It's exactly the size of what you could become. The growl was your own voice, calling from the size you haven't grown into yet.

Uniqueness · a B-value

Room III · Citrinitas, the yellowing

χάος

chaos · the yawning gap, first of all things in Hesiod

The Gap

Don't look. It's too big.

This room holds one thing: a size. It's where we keep the questions with no bottom. Why did they die. What's the point. Where does it all go. What happens to me.

It's the room existential intelligence was built for. Gardner's definition reads like a map of it: the biggest and smallest things there are, the meaning of a life, the meaning of a death. Most of us nail this door shut and decide we're just not deep like that.

Ask it something

Why me? Why me? Why—

That one's a verdict wearing a question mark. The dark gets heavier and the room gets smaller. Ask a better one.

The dark fills with stars. Thousands. It was always this big, and so is the part of you that can feel it.

Hesiod says the first thing that ever was is Chaos, the yawning gap, and everything else came out of it. Your lamp catches the dust hanging in the air, too. The infinite and the infinitesimal, Gardner's two ends, both in one room, both lit.

Meaningfulness · a B-value

On the road

ψυχή

psychē · breath, soul, butterfly

The Others Down Here

You're going to pass people. Everybody comes down here sometime, and some of them have been sitting in the same room for years.

Psyche came down here too. The goddess she'd crossed sent her to fetch a box from the queen of the dead, and a tower told her how to come back alive. Carry two honey cakes and two coins. You'll meet a lame man with a lame donkey who wants help with his load. A dead man in the river, reaching up for your hand. Old women at a loom, asking you to take a turn. Pass them all. Drop a cake helping them and you'll never get past the dog.

The myth is harder than I'd be. Here's the version I'd hand you.

Offer comfort in passing. A word, a light, a hand on the shoulder on your way through. Then keep walking. Their gold is in their own dark, and nobody can dig it for them. Set up camp in somebody else's room and you'll end up eating the pomegranate. Persephone ate its seeds down here and had to come back every year.

Leave a light

The one carrying a load

“Set some of that down. It's heavier than it has to be.”

The one in the river

“I see you. Keep going. It's in there.”

The one at the loom

“There's a new thread in that pattern. Pull it.”

Their way out runs through their own rooms. Yours runs through yours.

The guardian

κύων

kyōn · dog; kynikos, dog-like, gives us cynic

The Dog at the Door

Between you and the deepest room sits a dog with three heads.

Hesiod gave him fifty. Later tellers settled on three. Hesiod also left us his worst habit: he wags at everybody coming in, and eats anybody trying to leave.

  • Depression. Latin deprimere, to press down. It sits on your chest.
  • Rumination. Latin ruminari, to chew the cud. The same mouthful, all night long.
  • Cynicism. Greek kynikos, dog-like. It sneers at everything you love so nothing can let it down.

All three eat the same thing: your curiosity. Watch the lamp dim when they breathe on it.

Easy to walk in. Hard to walk out. That's the dog.

The myths left three ways past him.

Get past the dog

Rumination

The Sibyl got Aeneas past with a honey cake. Rumination wants something to chew, so hand it something sweet: sleep, a real meal, a walk, the song you love. Small care. Cura again. The chewing head lies down.

Cynicism

Orpheus played, and all three mouths hung open and forgot to bite. Cynicism can't hold up against something beautiful and true. Play it one. The sneering head lies down.

Depression

Press and hold. Heavy things take a minute.

Heracles hauled the dog up into daylight with his bare hands, and Homer says he didn't go alone: Hermes and Athena guided him. Bring it up where there's light, and bring someone. If it's been on your chest a long time, your Hermes and Athena are a doctor, a counselor, and the friend who picks up at 2 a.m. Even Heracles took the help.

The dog lies down. He always could. Behind him, the door to the deepest room stands open.

The deepest room · Rubedo, the reddening

ζώνη

zōnē · belt, girdle

The Will

At the very bottom, something is buried. It's black, and it's warm.

The alchemists called it the black sun, sol niger: the darkest stage of the work, the one that comes right before the gold.

I've been turning this one over for a long time. There's a strip of your brain called the anterior midcingulate cortex. Cingulate is Latin, from cingulum, a belt, because it wraps the middle of the brain like one. When you gird yourself for something hard, that's the belt you're cinching.

In 2013, neurologists at Stanford sent a small current into that spot in two patients who already had electrodes in place to map their seizures. Both felt the same thing. One said it was like driving toward a storm on the far side of a hill, on a tire going flat. Worried, and set on getting through. In his words: push harder, push harder, push harder. They put the feeling in the paper's title: the will to persevere.

Superagers, people in their sixties, seventies and eighties with the memory of people decades younger, tend to keep this region thick. A team at Mass General has made the case that it's a hub of tenacity, and that it may be trainable; regular exercise looks like it grows it. Andrew Huberman went further on his podcast with David Goggins: maybe this is the seat of the will to live. The ice-bath crowd took it from there.

The science is young, and the slogans run ahead of it. I'll keep the image anyway. The will to live has an address in the body, and it gets stronger every time you walk into the dark on purpose.

Every room you just walked into was a rep.

Press and hold. That's the belt.

The black sun turns gold in your hands.

Aliveness · a B-value

The return

ἠώς

ēōs · dawn

The Sun Spends the Night Down Here

Lift it.

It's heavier than it looks, and warm. The ceiling peels back. The bats turn back into birds. The gold in your hands climbs over the ridge and becomes the morning.

The Egyptians said the sun sails through the underworld every night. Their books of the night count twelve hours and twelve gates, with a serpent going after the boat along the way. Every morning it comes back up as a scarab, rolling the new day over the hill.

Every sunrise you've ever seen was something coming back from the underworld.

The mountain and the cave were one mountain the whole time. Longinus put the sublime at the top. Edmund Burke, seventeen centuries later, found it in the dark: obscurity, depth, terror held at a safe distance. They were both right. Height and depth are one line, and you're the one who travels it.

Campbell called the one who makes it back the master of the two worlds. The last stage on his map is the freedom to live.
