The so-called Count

aliasComte de Saint-Germain

He also went by Marquis de Montferrat, Comte Bellamarre, Chevalier Schoening, Count Weldon, Comte Soltikoff, Graf Tzarogy and Prinz Ragoczy.

He walks into the record in 1745 already grown, rich and nameless. He walks out of it in 1784 under a burial entry that admits it doesn't know who it's burying. Everything in between, he let other people write.

I · ὄνομα · onoma, a name

Goes by the Name Of

London, December 1745. A Stuart prince's army has just turned back at Derby, a few days' march from the capital, and the city is jumpy about foreigners. Officers pick up one who calls himself Count St. Germain.

Horace Walpole, son of the late prime minister, wrote the arrest up for a friend in Florence.

“…will not tell who he is, or whence, but professes that he does not go by his right name.”
Horace Walpole to Horace Mann, 9 December 1745

He sings, Walpole goes on. He plays the violin wonderfully. He composes. London fills in the rest: he's Italian, or Spanish, or a Pole. A priest, a fiddler, a vast nobleman. A man who married a fortune in Mexico and ran off to Constantinople with her jewels.

Nothing is proved. They let him go, and he stays in town telling people about the time he was arrested as a spy. Walpole takes that as proof he's no gentleman.

Over the next forty years he goes by Marquis de Montferrat, Comte Bellamarre, Chevalier Schoening, Count Weldon, Comte Soltikoff, Graf Tzarogy, Prinz Ragoczy. Ask him which one is real.

The frame stays empty. The one painting of him anyone knows of is lost. An engraving made from it in 1783 is all that's left of his face.

Two of those names are one name. Spell Rákóczi the old German way, RAGOTZY, and shuffle the letters: TZAROGY.

Late in life he told a German prince what the shuffle was hiding. He was a son of Francis II Rákóczi, the rebel Prince of Transylvania. He never proved it. A margrave he'd stayed with looked into it, doubted it, and the two parted ways.

A name you choose is a story you get to start.

Each one is born at its full height and burns down to nothing at its owner's death. The gold candle in the middle is the count's. II · χρόνος · chronos, time

The Man Who Never Dies

By the late 1750s he's at Versailles, a regular in Madame de Pompadour's rooms. Casanova says the king gave him rooms at the château of Chambord for a laboratory.

Pompadour's lady's maid, Madame du Hausset, kept notes. He looked about fifty, she wrote. Neither fat nor thin. Dressed simply and in good taste, with diamonds on his rings, his watch, his snuffbox and his shoe buckles.

An old lady at court, Madame de Gergy, swore she had known him in Venice years before, and that he hadn't changed at all. Pompadour asked him about it. It was not impossible, he said, that he was over a hundred. It was also possible Madame de Gergy was in her dotage.

He could describe Francis I, two centuries dead, the way you'd describe a man you had watched across a room. His own explanation, as Madame du Hausset set it down: a good memory, and he sometimes amused himself by letting people believe he'd lived in the old days.

Drag the years. Everyone at that table burns down. On paper, his candle never does.

Voltaire, writing to Frederick the Great, gave the legend its tagline:

“a man who never dies, and who knows everything.”
Voltaire to Frederick II, 15 April 1760

He was joking. In the same letter he has the count dining with the fathers of the Council of Trent, two hundred years before. The joke outlived Voltaire.

One more thing people noticed. At a dinner in Paris in 1757, Casanova watched him talk from the first course to the last and eat nothing. As a talker, Casanova wrote, he was unequalled. His boasts, Casanova added, were barefaced lies.

He never said he was immortal. He let you do the arithmetic.

First, a cut diamond seen from above, with a dark flaw near its table that fades away; beside it a ledger of three bars: 240 louis flawed, 400 louis clean, and 380 louis, what the jeweller paid. Then a small iron brazier of coals with a silver coin on top and a blowpipe at its side. Holding the button heats the coals until the coin turns gold. Watch his hands shows the trick Casanova suspected: the silver coin going up a lace cuff, and a gold one waiting in the palm.

III · χρυσοποιία · chrysopoeia, gold-making

The Coin in the Coals

Louis XV owned a diamond with a flaw in it. Flawed, it was worth 240 louis. Clean, 400. Could the count, the king wondered, put the difference in his pocket?

A month later the stone came back wrapped in asbestos cloth, clean, its weight barely changed. The king's jeweller paid 380 louis for it. The king gave back the money and kept the stone as a curiosity. The count must be worth millions, he decided. The count neither confirmed nor denied having the secret.

A century and a half later the Scottish writer Andrew Lang guessed it wasn't the same stone at all.

Tournai, 1764. Casanova finds him in a room full of retorts, wearing a beard two inches long and setting up a hat factory for Count Cobenzl, the Austrian minister in Brussels. Casanova arrives a skeptic.

Casanova hands over a silver twelve-sols piece. The count sets it on the coals with a small black grain, and blows.

Hold the button and blow with him.

When it cools, it's gold. Casanova is sure his silver piece was smuggled away and a gold one slipped in. He writes the count down as a celebrated and learned impostor. Watch the hands.

“Those that are capable of entertaining doubts of my art are not worthy to speak to me.”
The count to Casanova, as Casanova tells it

The oldest alchemy works on the audience.

IV · ξήριον · xērion, a drying powder for wounds

The Tea

Madame de Gergy told Pompadour the count's elixir had made her feel twenty-four again. Why not give some to the king? The count looked alarmed. He would have to be mad, he said, to give the king an unknown drug.

Elixir has come a long way to get here. Greek xērion, a dry powder for wounds. Arabic al-iksīr, the powder alchemists hoped would perfect a metal. Medieval Latin elixir, a cure for everything. Then the bottle on every quack's cart.

In Tournai he offered Casanova fifteen pills that would cure him in three days, and showed off a white liquid he called the universal spirit of nature.

What actually survived him was a tea. In nineteenth-century Germany and Denmark, people drank something called Saint-Germain tea. The leaf that did the work was senna. Steep it and see.

Senna is a laxative.

The elixir of life, as sold, kept things moving.

V · φήμη · phēmē, a rumor, a voice going around

Tell It Again

Virgil drew Rumor as a monster with an eye, a tongue and an ear under every feather, who grows stronger the farther she travels.

vires acquirit eundo
Virgil, Aeneid 4.175 · she gathers strength by going

Watch his age do it. Every time someone tells the story, tap Tell it again.

Look at who's talking. A maid's memory. A rival's memoirs. A count's diary, a baron's gossip, a comedian's act, an editor's footnote. One number came from the man himself with a source attached: 88.

The footnote even gave him a valet, who said he had only had the honor of serving his master for five hundred years.

Retellings don't only grow. They drift. In du Hausset, Madame de Gergy knew him in Venice. By 1912 a biographer had her down as the Countess de Georgy, and the meeting dated to 1710.

That was the method, if it was a method. He left a gap the exact shape of a miracle, and everyone who passed through it came out with a bigger number.

A hand writes the entry line by line: Deceased on February 27th, buried on March 2nd, 1784, the so-called Comte de St. Germain and Weldon. Further information not known. A small slip of paper lists the estate: about 82 reichsthaler and 13 shillings, clothing, linen, small effects. Gold, jewels and elixir are each listed, and struck through.

VI · σῆμα · sēma, a sign; also a grave-mound

Further Information Not Known

1779. He turns up in Schleswig, on the Baltic, and finds a last patron: Prince Charles of Hesse-Kassel, who sets him up in an empty factory at Eckernförde to work on dyes for cloth.

He tells the prince he's 88. The prince calls him one of the greatest philosophers who ever lived.

He dies at Eckernförde on 27 February 1784, while the prince is away in Kassel. The clerk at St. Nicolai writes him into the burial register.

“Deceased on February 27th, buried on March 2nd, 1784, the so-called Comte de St. Germain and Weldon. Further information not known.”
Burial register, St. Nicolai, Eckernförde · as usually translated

The town counts up what he left. About 82 reichsthaler. Clothes, linen, a few small things. No gold. No jewels. No elixir.

Even the record hedges.

VII · φάσμα · phasma, an apparition

He Would Not Stay Buried

Then he kept turning up.

In 1836 a book appeared: the memoirs of the Comtesse d'Adhémar, a lady of Marie Antoinette's court. In them the count warns the queen of a bloody revolution, then keeps calling on the countess, unchanged, for years after his burial. The book was a forgery, written by Étienne-Léon de Lamothe-Langon, who made a career of inventing other people's memoirs.

The Theosophists took him up next. Helena Blavatsky called him the greatest adept Europe had seen in centuries. Annie Besant said she met him in 1896.

In August 1930, on the slopes of Mount Shasta, a mining engineer named Guy Ballard said he met the count, by then an Ascended Master. His 1934 book launched the I AM movement, which still teaches in the count's name. For believers he is a living teacher, and a parish register has nothing to say about that.

In January 1972 a Frenchman named Richard Chanfray went on French television, said he was the Comte de Saint-Germain, and turned lead into gold on camera. The last ring on the line is you, reading about him.

The grave is in the register. The name kept walking.

VIII · βάσανος · basanos, a touchstone; later, any hard test

Assay

Goldsmiths had a way to tell gold from brass without melting anything. Draw the metal across a black stone and read the streak. The Greeks called the stone basanos. The word grew to mean any hard test.

Here are nine things people say about him. Rub each one on the stone.

  • ☉ Goldon the record at the time, from someone in a position to know
  • ♀ Brassone witness's word, or his own, never proved
  • ♄ Leadrumor or forgery, with no witness at all

Three streaks of gold: a fiddler, a joke of Voltaire's, a burial. That's the man. Everything else is the myth, and the myth is the part still working.

The dust of every story told about him gathers into the silhouette of an eighteenth-century gentleman in a tricorn hat and a tied wig. The gilt frame from the beginning closes around it. At the end the dust lifts away like smoke and the frame is empty again.

μῦθος · mythos, a thing said; a story

Leave the Gap

He never told anyone he was immortal. He described a dead king's face, and smiled. He let an old woman's memory do the arithmetic. Asked about the diamond, he neither confirmed nor denied.

He made himself out of what was at hand: a violin, a good coat, diamonds on his buckles, a laboratory, and silence at exactly the right moment.

Everyone else did the rest. A maid, a rival, a philosopher, a comedian, a forger, a mystic, a man on television. Two hundred and forty years of people finishing his story for him.

That's the lever under every myth. Leave a gap the shape of a miracle and someone will fill it. It cuts both ways. The same gap let a forger put words in his mouth, and a stranger wear his name on TV.

Tell your own story while you're holding the pen.

Further information not known.

St. Nicolai · Eckernförde · 2 March 1784

A note on the assay

The gold, brass and lead verdicts are this piece's judgment calls, made from the sources above.
